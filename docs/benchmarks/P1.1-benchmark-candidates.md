## Scope

This dossier records published candidates for a first bounded acoustic-force study. The project owner clarified that AURA's long-term goal is prescribed acceleration in microgravity and approved a measurable ground-based force check as the starting point, with further benchmark searches as the project advances. The original liquid/microparticle candidates do not represent the target body or medium. This screen prioritizes air experiments on centimeter-scale bodies with directly measured forces, while distinguishing a force-model benchmark from evidence that AURA can achieve its eventual microgravity objective.

## Candidate A — viscous radiation and streaming velocities in a rectangular microchannel

**Primary source:** R. Barnkob, P. Augustsson, T. Laurell, and H. Bruus, “Acoustic radiation- and streaming-induced microparticle velocities determined by microparticle image velocimetry in an ultrasound symmetry plane,” *Physical Review E* 86, 056307 (2012), [doi:10.1103/PhysRevE.86.056307](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevE.86.056307). The authors' [full-text version](https://arxiv.org/html/1208.6534) is available.

- **Regime and model:** dilute spherical polystyrene particles in a resonant, one-dimensional transverse standing-wave field in a silicon/glass water microchannel. The observable combines time-averaged radiation-force drift and acoustic-streaming Stokes drag. The paper uses a small-particle viscous radiation-force expression, a streaming model, and wall-drag corrections; its Eqs. (2), (11)–(15) define the force/velocity relation and fitted profile.
- **Geometry and field:** channel length 35 mm, width 377 µm, height 157 µm; half-wave condition `lambda/2 = w` at 1.940 MHz (or full-wave `lambda = w` at 3.900 MHz). Measurements were made in the horizontal ultrasound symmetry plane. Conditions include water and a 0.75:0.25 water:glycerol mixture.
- **Material values at 25 °C:** polystyrene density 1050 kg m⁻³, compressibility 249 TPa⁻¹, contrast factor `Phi = 0.17` in water; water density 997 kg m⁻³, sound speed 1497 m s⁻¹, dynamic viscosity 0.890 mPa s, and compressibility 448 TPa⁻¹. Measured particle diameters span about 0.6–10.2 µm, with reported diameter spreads in Table 3.
- **Primary observable:** fitted transverse particle-velocity amplitude `u_a` versus particle radius/diameter, with spatial form `u_y = u_a sin(2 k y)` away from the side walls. The benchmark can also test the reported radiation-to-streaming velocity ratios in Table 5 and the fitted/endpoint parameters in Table 4.
- **Reported references:** for MQ1 (water, 1.940 MHz), Table 4(b) reports `E_ac = 32.436 ± 1.282 J m⁻³` and streaming coefficient `s = 0.182 ± 0.008`; Table 5 reports `u_a(0.6 µm)/u_a(10 µm) = 0.020` and `u_a(10 µm)/u_a(0.6 µm) = 49.6`. The diameter-scaling ratio for the 0.6, 5, and 10 µm classes is 0.294 against the idealized `(5/10)^2 = 0.25` prediction. The authors report an 8–18% deviation across series for that ratio. Table 4(a) instead fits all particle sizes and returns different `E_ac` and `s`; the two extraction methods must not be conflated.
- **Uncertainty and limitations:** particle-size dispersion and fit uncertainty are reported; the authors identify possible density/compressibility variation, non-Gaussian size distributions, concentration-driven particle interactions, and viscosity changes as uncertainty sources. Figure 8 states that the standard deviation of `u_a/u_0` is smaller than the plotted symbol size; the diameter spreads are drawn as horizontal bars. Table 5 gives the endpoint ratio 49.6 without an uncertainty interval for that ratio. The project owner has no access to the APS supplement. The public article and its plotted figures are therefore the available reference; any values recovered from a figure must carry separate digitization uncertainty and must not be presented as the article's replicate-level uncertainty. The 0.6 µm and 10 µm endpoints are treated as streaming-dominated and radiation-dominated, respectively, to estimate some ratios. Wall interactions, streaming, channel boundaries, and temperature dependence make this richer but less isolated than a force-only test.
- **Candidate use:** strong experimental candidate for a microchannel particle-velocity benchmark and cross-check of force-plus-drag implementation; it is not a direct isolated-force measurement.

## Candidate B — three-dimensional particle trajectories and velocity field

**Primary source:** P. B. Muller et al., “Ultrasound-induced acoustophoretic motion of microparticles in three dimensions,” *Physical Review E* 88, 023006 (2013), [doi:10.1103/PhysRevE.88.023006](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevE.88.023006). The authors' [full-text version](https://arxiv.org/html/1303.0201) is available.

- **Regime and model:** measured 3D motion of spherical polystyrene particles in a resonant rectangular microchannel. The predictions combine radiation force and streaming-induced drag, with an analytical channel model and a numerical comparison. The reported experimental benchmark therefore tests a coupled flow-and-particle response rather than force alone.
- **Geometry and field:** silicon/glass channel length 35 mm, width 377 µm, height 157 µm; transverse half-wave resonance at 1.94 MHz. The setup was temperature-controlled. For 5 µm particles, 10 experiments yielded 111 trajectories at `U_pp = 0.91 V`; for 0.5 µm particles, four experiments yielded 731 trajectories at `U_pp = 1.62 V`.
- **Material and measurement values:** nominal particle diameters 5.33 µm (SD 0.09) and 0.537 µm (PDI 0.005); density 1050 kg m⁻³ and compressibility 249 TPa⁻¹. Reported position uncertainty is ±1 µm in `z` and less than ±0.1 µm in `x` and `y`.
- **Primary observable:** 3D trajectories and binned velocity vectors in the channel cross-section. Figure 5 compares the 0.5 µm experiment, analytical prediction, and numerical solution on a 37×15 array of 10 µm bins; Fig. 4 shows trajectories. The model's primary reported data include velocity magnitude and direction as spatial fields.
- **Reported reference values:** for the 0.5 µm case, Figure 5 reports maximum measured speed 63 µm s⁻¹ and analytical/numerical maximum 59 µm s⁻¹. The authors report experimental speeds approximately 20% higher on average than the analytical prediction; the mean experimental error is about 1 µm s⁻¹ (relative average about 4%), and the experiment and analytical prediction do not agree within the combined stated error. The acoustic energy density for the 0.5 µm case is calibrated from the 5 µm case and voltage scaling as `65 ± 2 J m⁻³`; for the 5 µm calibration, `20.6 ± 0.7 J m⁻³` is reported.
- **Uncertainty and limitations:** reported uncertainty includes position tracking, standard error of mean by bins, and uncertainty in acoustic energy-density calibration. Side-wall bins lack experimental data. Five selected near-centerline 5 µm trajectories calibrate acoustic energy density, which creates a calibration dependency between cases. A persistent ~20% experiment/model difference is a result to preserve and investigate, not a tolerance to waive. Raw numerical arrays and the availability of trajectory files need confirmation; figures alone may require digitization.
- **Candidate use:** strong candidate if a 3D particle-motion observable is desired and the first implementation is allowed to include acoustic streaming and channel-wall effects. Its complexity and the reported discrepancy may make it unsuitable as the minimal first benchmark.

## Candidate C — analytical viscous radiation force on a small sphere

**Primary source:** M. Settnes and H. Bruus, “Forces acting on a small particle in an acoustical field in a viscous fluid,” *Physical Review E* 85, 016327 (2012), [doi:10.1103/PhysRevE.85.016327](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevE.85.016327). The [published-paper PDF](https://bruus-lab.dk/TMF/publications/Bruus/Bruus_101.pdf) is available from the authors' research group.

- **Regime and model:** first-order scattering plus viscous acoustic-boundary-layer correction for a compressible sphere in a liquid. The long-wavelength conditions require both particle radius `a` and viscous penetration depth `delta` to be much smaller than wavelength `lambda`; the model has no restrictions on `delta/a` within that asymptotic limit. Eqs. (47)–(50) provide standing- and traveling-wave force expressions.
- **Published numerical case:** 5 µm diameter Pyrex sphere in water at 1.5 MHz under a planar traveling wave, with acoustic energy density 100 J m⁻³. The paper reports `a = 2.5 µm`, `delta/a = 0.23`, `k = 4.24 × 10³ m⁻¹`, density ratio 2.23, imaginary dipole coefficient `f₂ᵢ = 0.058`, radiation force about 1.2 pN, and terminal Stokes speed about 25 µm s⁻¹. The paper's comparison with standard inviscid theory gives a force roughly `10⁻⁶` as large and speed below 0.1 nm s⁻¹ for the cited setup.
- **Available parameters:** Table I provides water and Pyrex density, compressibility, sound speed, and viscosity. For water at 20 °C it reports `rho = 998.2 kg m⁻³`, `kappa = 456 TPa⁻¹`, `c = 1482 m s⁻¹`, `eta = 1.002 mPa s`; for Pyrex it reports `rho = 2230 kg m⁻³`, `kappa = 27.8 TPa⁻¹`, `c = 5674 m s⁻¹`.
- **Primary observable:** calculated time-averaged force along the traveling-wave vector as a function of particle radius, frequency, acoustic energy density, and `delta/a`. In a standing-wave variant, Eq. (50) defines a spatially varying force via the acoustic contrast factor.
- **Uncertainty and limitations:** the paper presents analytical theory and illustrative calculations, not a direct experimental data set or a numerical uncertainty interval. It is straightforward to reproduce its closed-form case but using the same equations as the reference would not be an independent validation. The paper discusses a sign discrepancy in a prior Doinikov expression and attributes it as possibly a misprint; this is a literature caution, not a reported erratum to Settnes and Bruus.
- **Candidate use:** a useful analytical known-answer test for a force-model implementation after the modeling regime is fixed; it cannot alone validate the physical force law against independent measurements.

## Cross-source notes and unresolved checks

- Candidate A and B have substantial author/method overlap and both depend on the same microchannel family; they should not be presented as fully independent confirmations of each other.
- Candidate A's Table 5 describes velocity ratios, not direct force measurements. Any comparison must keep the force-to-velocity conversion, Stokes drag, wall correction, and streaming contribution explicit.
- Candidate B publishes a useful discrepancy: its measured small-particle field is around 20% above the analytical mean, outside reported combined uncertainty. Preserve it as a benchmark outcome rather than adopting 20% as a project tolerance.
- Candidate C provides a clean closed-form calculation but no independent experimental output. It can be an analytical reference, not the sole published validation.
- Barnkob et al. discuss an earlier 500 kHz standing-wave experiment by K. Yasuda and T. Kamakura (1997; DOI [10.1063/1.119395](https://doi.org/10.1063/1.119395)) and state that their results do not support its reported sub-5 µm size trend. The earlier paper's full parameter set and data have not yet been checked, so it is a provenance/contradiction lead, not a scored candidate here.
- P1.1 source review checked the public full text and figures. The source's reported symbol-size statement is useful for a conservative figure-reading bound, but it does not provide a confidence interval for Table 5's endpoint ratio.

## P1.2 — Candidate scoring

The plan names five screening dimensions but does not assign weights. For a transparent screen, score each dimension from 0 (poor/unavailable) to 2 (strong/available) and weight all dimensions equally. In response to owner review, `regime match` requires consistency with the intended medium (air), body scale and free-body purpose; it is not enough that a paper uses ultrasound. This ordinal screen organizes evidence; it is not a measurement of scientific truth.

| Candidate | Parameter completeness | Reference data | Air and body-scale match | Implementation tractability | Independent observable | Total / 10 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| A — Barnkob et al. 2012, 2D micro-PIV | 2 | 1 | 0 | 1 | 2 | **6** |
| B — Muller et al. 2013, 3D particle motion | 2 | 1 | 0 | 0 | 2 | **5** |
| C — Settnes & Bruus 2012, analytical sphere force | 2 | 2 | 0 | 2 | 0 | **6** |

**Scoring basis**

- **A:** geometry, materials, field, particle-size distributions, and uncertainty sources are reported. Numeric summaries (Tables 4–5) are accessible in the paper, but its acquisition table and measured velocity fields are in an APS subscription-required supplement. A useful first case must include force, Stokes drag, streaming, and wall corrections, so it is more work than a force-only calculation. Its micro-PIV measurement is independent of AURA's implementation.
- **B:** the geometry, material, actuation, particle classes, and measurement uncertainty are well specified, and measured 3D fields are published. Reproducing the full cross-sectional streaming solution and 3D particle velocities is relatively demanding. Experimental velocities are independent observables, but the paper reports a systematic mean experiment/model difference near 20%, so this cannot be turned into a pass threshold without resolving the discrepancy.
- **C:** the paper gives a closed-form force expression and an explicit numeric Pyrex/water example, making it easy to reproduce. It is a traveling-wave case rather than the standing-wave microchannel regime, and its numeric reference is produced by the same analytical theory family that a force implementation would use; it is therefore an analytical known-answer case, not an independent experimental validation.

None of these three original candidates is recommended as the primary AURA benchmark: their liquid medium and micron-scale objects do not match the reviewed target. Candidate C may still serve as a narrow analytical code check. Candidate B may be useful for micro-PIV/streaming work if that subsystem later needs validation. They cannot support claims about air levitation or macroscopic-body performance.

## P1.3–P1.7 — Approved starting point; exit work remains

The owner approved this initial benchmark on 2026-10-02 (see [DEC-002](../decisions/DEC-002-initial-measurable-force-benchmark.md)); P1 remains in REVIEW until the source uncertainty and comparison rule are complete. The benchmark is the single-transducer force measurement reported by M. A. B. Andrade, A. L. Bernassau, and J. C. Adamowski, “Acoustic levitation of a large solid sphere,” *Applied Physics Letters* 109, 044101 (2016), [DOI: 10.1063/1.4959862](https://doi.org/10.1063/1.4959862). The authors tested a 50 mm expanded-polystyrene sphere with mass 1.46 g in air. They drove one 20 mm-face transducer at 25,230 Hz and 15 µm displacement, moved it on a stage, and measured the sphere's axial acoustic force on an electronic scale as the gap changed. The paper compares that measured force-versus-gap curve with a finite-element calculation. The same paper demonstrates free levitation of the sphere with three transducers in a tripod arrangement.

| P1 design item | Proposed definition |
|---|---|
| Primary question | Under the published single-source conditions, does AURA predict the measured axial force on a centimeter-scale body in air as the source-to-body gap changes? |
| Body and medium | 50 mm expanded-polystyrene sphere; mass 1.46 g; air at 25 °C (reported density 1.18 kg m⁻³ and sound speed 346 m s⁻¹). |
| Source case | One 20 mm circular transducer; 25,230 Hz; 15 µm face-displacement amplitude; source-to-sphere gap swept from 0.1 to 30 mm in 0.1 mm increments. |
| Primary observable | Axial acoustic radiation force versus gap, with force sign and transducer displacement convention matched to the source. |
| Reference | Experimental electronic-scale curve in Fig. 5 of the paper. The publication's numerical curve is useful context but is not an independent experimental reference. |
| Uncertainty | Digitize the experimental plot and add graph-reading uncertainty. The source attributes model/measurement differences to experimental uncertainty and simplifying assumptions; a complete pointwise uncertainty budget still needs review. Do not set a percentage tolerance before recovering the plotted data and uncertainty sources. |
| Stop rule | If the source's force curve, source amplitude convention, gap coordinate, or usable uncertainty cannot be recovered well enough to make a fair comparison, stop and return to the owner instead of inventing data or widening tolerance. |
| Resource estimate | This is a fixed-geometry force sweep over one coordinate, not a controller or full-array optimization. A converged acoustic-scattering calculation is more demanding than a particle formula, but the domain is compact; estimate mesh and runtime after selecting the solver and before a scientific run. |

**What this would establish:** a successful comparison would support the force calculation for this air, sphere, frequency, source, and gap range. It would be a useful measured anchor before predicting forces in AURA's target setting.

**What it would not establish:** this single-source measurement does not test multi-element control, arbitrary acceleration directions, long-duration stability, multiple bodies, or microgravity operation. It is a model-validation step toward AURA's goal, not evidence that the goal has been achieved. Passing it would allow a later array-level benchmark.

**Scale caveat:** the sphere is 5 cm across but weighs only 1.46 g because it is expanded polystyrene. It is closer to AURA's intended size and medium than a microparticle, but it does not represent a dense or heavy target body. That limitation must remain attached to any result.

### Follow-on candidate for array-level stability

S. Inoue et al., “Acoustical boundary hologram for macroscopic rigid-body levitation,” *Journal of the Acoustical Society of America* 145(1), 328 (2019), [DOI: 10.1121/1.5087130](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.5087130), demonstrated levitation in air of a 30 mm, 0.6 g polystyrene sphere and a roughly 50 mm octahedron using a 40 kHz phased array, and reported force-displacement results. This is closer to AURA's multi-element manipulation objective, but it uses a much larger array and its model neglects air viscosity. The article also reports incomplete stability in the sphere trials. It is therefore a suitable later stability benchmark, not the simplest first force check.

### Decision scope and benchmark evolution

The owner's approval is limited to using this **5 cm lightweight sphere in air** as AURA's first measurable force-model check. The first computer comparison asks whether the predicted acoustic push follows the published scale-measured force curve as the gap changes. It is a ground-based first step toward microgravity, not a reproduction of microgravity and not proof of AURA's full objective.

As AURA advances, each new capability requires a fresh literature and measurement search. The array-level restoring-force case from Inoue et al. is a candidate for that later step, not an automatically approved next benchmark. New body mass/material, array geometry, acceleration target or microgravity claim requires its own bounded case and evidence. Do not extrapolate the 1.46 g foam-sphere result to heavier or denser bodies.

**Remaining P1 work:** recover the plotted force points; verify measurement and digitization uncertainty; define comparison, stop and resource rules; record the experiment definition. Do not implement a solver during P1. After P1 PASS, follow the later P2–P5 gates before force-model implementation.
