## Evidence-derived MQ1 constraints

The primary source describes a silicon-glass chip with a water channel of length 35 mm, width 377 µm, and height 157 µm. Figure 1 centers the transverse coordinate, with channel edges at `y = ±w/2`; under that convention its ideal field `p₁(r) = pₐ cos[k(y − w/2)]`, with `k = π/w`, has a pressure node at `y = 0` and zero normal pressure gradient at both channel edges. MQ1 is listed at 1.940 MHz. The same source lists water at 25 °C with `c₀ = 1497 m/s`, and states that boundary-driven streaming depends on the rectangular walls and thermoviscous effects. See Barnkob et al., [author manuscript, Figure 1 and Sections II–IV, Eqs. (1), (2), (6)–(15), and Table II](https://arxiv.org/html/1208.6534).

These reported quantities do not close under a homogeneous-water, exact half-wave calculation:

| Calculation | Result | Interpretation |
|---|---:|---|
| `c₀ / f` using 1497 m/s and 1.940 MHz | 771.65 µm wavelength | Conditional homogeneous-water wavelength |
| `c₀ / (2f)` | 385.82 µm half wavelength | 8.82 µm (2.34%) above the reported 377 µm width |
| `2 f w` | 1462.76 m/s implied speed for exact half-wave | 34.24 m/s (2.29%) below the reported `c₀` |
| `w / (c₀/f)` | 0.4886 | Reported width is not exactly one half of this conditional wavelength |

This arithmetic is not evidence of an error in the experiment. It identifies unresolved effective resonance, geometry, and material inputs. Do not silently substitute an adjusted sound speed or frequency. The article's ideal cosine with the figure's centered coordinate remains a valid manufactured reference under its own declared half-wave assumption, but matching it is not a reconstruction of the resonant silicon-glass device.

The article further reports that its measured symmetry-plane particle velocity combines radiation-force response and acoustic-streaming advection; the two mechanisms vary with particle size. It states that the simple parallel-plate streaming approximation is modified by rectangular side walls and thermoviscous effects. Thus MQ1 velocity is not a direct acoustic-force or acceleration measurement. The raw field-data and calibration gaps recorded in [LIT-02](../benchmarks/water-reference-selection.md) remain unchanged.

## Method selection for the DEC-002 air case

Hasegawa et al. (1985) derive the nearfield velocity potential for a rigid sphere centered on the axis of a circular piston in an infinite rigid baffle as spherical-harmonic series. Their Eqs. (1)–(12) build the piston incident field and its diffraction integrals; Eqs. (10)–(20) add sphere scattering. The paper gives the standard sound-hard coefficient for modes other than the translating `n=1` mode and derives a distinct `n=1` coefficient when the sphere is allowed to translate. For AURA's P4 field-only model, the stationary sound-hard boundary is imposed independently at every order, including `n=1`; that `n=1` stationary coefficient follows by applying zero normal velocity to the paper's boundary condition, not by reusing its freely translating coefficient. This is an explicit model derivation, not an experimentally supported EPS boundary property. The article presents the framework as exact for its harmonic, centered, baffled-piston assumptions and does not require numerical surface integration. Its sample computations use `kR=30`, `ka=5`, and several axial center distances; these examples do not establish convergence at AURA parameters. The J-STAGE record is [10.1250/ast.6.9](https://doi.org/10.1250/ast.6.9); the reviewed primary PDF is the six-page 1985 article, pp. 9–14.

**NUM-01 method selection:** use numerical evaluation of this piston/sphere spherical-harmonic series as the first P4 backend, restricted to a coaxial circular baffled piston, homogeneous lossless linear air, one frequency, and a centered spherical body. Derive the stationary sound-hard coefficient at all harmonic orders from the zero-normal-velocity boundary condition; do not use the source's freely translating-sphere `n=1` coefficient. This is the closest mathematical match among the reviewed candidates to the DEC-002 ideal source/sphere geometry and avoids introducing an approximate open-boundary truncation or near-gap surface-mesh interaction as the first method. It is an implementer method selection, not an owner-approved physical boundary/material claim.

The series backend must use a stable and explicit mapping from the article's `exp(+iωt)` convention to FIELD-1.0 `exp(−iωt)`, and must return complex pressure, fluid velocity, pressure gradient and mean intensity with coordinates and units. The stationary `n=1` coefficient and differentiated series identities require independent derivation and tests. First verify (a) the no-sphere piston field against an independently evaluated Rayleigh disk integral and published axis/far-field limits, (b) the rigid sphere under incident plane waves against the exact partial-wave solution and P3 plane-wave fields, and (c) the combined piston/sphere solution by harmonic-order convergence at the minimum and representative gaps. Compare pressure, velocity and required gradients, not only contour appearance. The later force integral and comparison with Andrade's measured curve remain downstream; this selection does not promote that measurement to an acceptance oracle.

This method is selected subject to NUM-02 resource and numerical stability preflight. Preflight must estimate and then measure special-function order, cancellation, truncation error, memory and runtime across `H=0.1–30 mm`; it must fail closed if no stable converged order is found under bounded CPU resources. If the series is unstable, too costly or fails independent checks, retain the failed evidence and reopen NUM-01 to compare Burton–Miller axisymmetric BEM or mapped-grid high-order finite differences. The older `a/(a+H)≈0.996` ratio belongs to the previously considered source-expansion route; it is not a proven convergence ratio or error bound for Hasegawa's series and must not be transferred to it.

The selected geometry remains idealized. DEC-002's source is a nominal single 25.23 kHz transducer with a 20 mm face and a 50 mm, 1.46 g EPS sphere. The exact series assumes an ideal circular piston in an infinite rigid baffle, a centered sphere, homogeneous harmonic medium, and the chosen sphere boundary branch. Actual air state, source velocity calibration, face/baffle geometry, EPS acoustic impedance, sphere support/motion condition and source measurement uncertainty are not fully frozen. Those gaps prevent claiming a measured-field validation, force-model validation or acceleration performance from P4.

The BEM review by van ’t Wout et al. (2021) describes acoustic Helmholtz transmission systems as dense after boundary discretization, notes the need for compression/preconditioning at scale, and emphasizes that no single formulation wins across all frequencies, materials, and geometries; see the [primary author manuscript](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.04594). This supports treating BEM as a later qualified branch rather than assuming that “BEM” names one universal solver. k-Wave's official [PML description](https://www.k-wave.org/documentation/example_na_controlling_the_pml.php) explains that its layer absorbs waves at computational edges and prevents FFT wraparound; that boundary behavior must not be mistaken for a reflecting microchannel wall.

For the periodic streamwise coordinate and Neumann transverse interval, the separated spectrum is `k² = (2πm/Lₓ)² + (nπ/w)²`, with nonnegative integer mode indices. The constant `(m,n) = (0,0)` is the zero eigenvalue. With `Lₓ = 892 µm` and `w = 377 µm`, the first streamwise wavenumber is `kₓ₁ = 2π/Lₓ`, while the target first transverse wavenumber is `kᵧ₁ = π/w`; their ratio is `kₓ₁/kᵧ₁ = 2w/Lₓ = 377/446 ≈ 0.84529`. Thus the `(1,0)` streamwise mode has lower positive `k` than the target `(0,1)` transverse mode. An eigensolver must request the target symmetry/subspace or identify modes by both indices; selecting only the smallest nonzero eigenvalue would answer a different question.

This domain is a deliberate manufactured overlap: it adds an independent discretized method while retaining an exact analytical comparison. The ideal sound-hard walls define the oracle problem; they do not model the physical silicon-glass chip's elastic response. If the next project question requires the *measured MQ1 field*, this recommendation is inadequate; obtain the source supplement and calibrated transducer/wall information, then revisit the boundary/scattering representation and a coupled FEM/BEM or validated alternative. Do not use this mode as a surrogate for the physical drive.

These are raw-array lower bounds, not predictions for the JSON/run-bundle encoding; measure actual serialization and peak process memory during NUM-06 and protect both with NUM-02 headroom. Python, SciPy, factorization/eigensolver workspace and allocator overhead also remain unmeasured. No runtime is predicted from these counts alone.

Proposed implementation guardrails for owner review are 39,345 grid nodes, 0.5 GiB process-memory cap, 64 MiB output cap, and 120 seconds wall time per eigen-solve on the documented local CPU profile. ## Selected P4 technical scope under DEC-005

This subsection scopes the selected air route and method comparison without changing accepted physical equations. Andrade et al. (2016) calculate the single-transducer/sphere field with a 2D axisymmetric linear-acoustic FEM model: a prescribed uniform harmonic displacement on the circular transducer face, an acoustically hard sphere, and PMLs at the outer air-domain boundaries. They obtain first-order pressure and velocity, then integrate the time-averaged momentum flux over a closed surface around the sphere to calculate axial radiation force. Their source computation is a useful method/domain description, not an independent validation target for another implementation. The owner-approved experimental observable remains the measured force-versus-gap curve; its uncertainty status and P1.3 INDETERMINATE rule are unchanged.

For the declared `f = 25,230 Hz`, `c₀ = 346 m/s`, and sphere radius `a = 25 mm`, `λ = c₀/f ≈ 13.714 mm` and `ka = 2πfa/c₀ ≈ 11.45` (`a/λ ≈ 1.823`). This is a wavelength-scale scattering problem, not a Rayleigh/Gor'kov small-particle case. The 10 mm source-face radius is also about `0.73λ`, so its finite-aperture field cannot be replaced by a plane wave without a separately justified approximation. The experimental gap range starts at `0.1 mm`, about `0.0073λ`; a candidate method must demonstrate that its geometry representation and near-boundary quadrature remain accurate there rather than relying on a coarse uniform mesh.

The source reports a 20 mm-diameter circular transducer, a 50 mm-diameter sphere and defines `H` as the distance from the transducer to the sphere surface ([Andrade et al.](https://doi.org/10.1063/1.4959862)). Under the conditional idealization of a planar face coaxial with a rigid sphere, the local clearance is `g(r)=H+a−√(a²−r²)`. At the experimental minimum `H=0.1 mm`, the sphere's geometric sag adds another 0.1 mm at `r=√(2aH−H²)≈2.234 mm`; at the 10 mm aperture edge, the gap is approximately `2.187 mm`. Thus only the central few millimeters of aperture face the minimum gap. This is a geometry-derived scale for near-gap mesh/quadrature refinement, not a claim about actual transducer flatness or the accuracy of either candidate solver.

There is also an evidence-coverage mismatch: the primary article describes its axisymmetric FEM pressure/force results over `H = 2–30 mm`, while its experiment and AURA's approved P1.3 reference span `H = 0.1–30 mm`. The checked derived CSVs contain 300 experimental samples; 19 points from `0.1–1.9 mm` lie outside the article's stated FEM interval, and the repository has digitized only the experimental (red) trace. These points are not source-FEM comparisons. An air-route solver intended to support the full later P1.3 comparison must establish its own numerical validity in this small-gap region; P4 itself still verifies fields only. Details and the no-omission rule are recorded in the [P1.3 specification](../benchmarks/P1.3-andrade-force-curve-specification.md#published-simulation-coverage-versus-measured-coverage).

## Selected method and separate water/recovery branches

**Selected method:** numerically evaluate the centered piston/rigid-sphere spherical-harmonic field series from Hasegawa et al. for the DEC-002 geometry, subject to NUM-02 preflight. This is independent of FEM and directly matches the ideal source/body configuration. Keep P3 analytical kernels as regression oracles, add piston-only Rayleigh and exact plane-wave/sphere partial-wave checks, then freeze harmonic-order convergence and resources before any solver-core construction. The method is not considered usable until the actual geometry shows stable convergence within a measured CPU/memory budget; if it fails, preserve the attempt and reopen the BEM/mapped-FD comparison. The water eigenmode remains a possible separate task, not part of the selected P4 path.
