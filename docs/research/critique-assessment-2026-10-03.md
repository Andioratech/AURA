# Assessment of the AURA Scientific-Risk Critique

**Prepared:** 2026-10-03 · **Purpose:** source-grounded assessment of the owner's nine-point critique · **Status:** assessment only; it does not change project scope, gate status or acceptance criteria

## Overall finding

The critique identifies real scientific and project risks. Its strongest points are the need to define any “pseudogravity” claim operationally, separate body-specific force/acceleration from gravity equivalence, measure open-loop field/body-force quality separately from feedback performance, test robustness against evidenced uncertainties, and keep feasibility distinct from usefulness. AURA's D00/D01 and plans already encode several of these boundaries, but they do not yet freeze a target-specific acceleration acceptance metric or claim hierarchy.

Two parts need precision. First, a force that depends on position does not make prescribed acceleration logically impossible: a designed field can have an approximately constant force over a declared region, and a controller may compensate residual variation. Those are different results that must be reported separately. Second, “force requires gradients” is a useful shorthand for some small-particle potential models and traps, not a universal statement across body sizes and scattering regimes. A general rigid-body calculation must account for incident-plus-scattered fields and momentum/radiation stress on a declared surface.

## Point-by-point assessment

| Critique point | Assessment | Evidence and project consequence |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Controlled acoustic force is not automatically gravity or general pseudogravity | **Correct; central claim boundary.** | D00 defines acoustic pseudogravity as mechanically induced acceleration and says it is not gravity generation; D01 distinguishes body/material/geometry-dependent acoustic forces from gravitational coupling. Primary models illustrate regime dependence: Gor'kov's potential is for a small particle in an ideal fluid, while Inoue et al. compute force and torque on a macroscopic rigid body from the total field and surface loading. Wang et al.'s standing-wave calculation for elastic core-shell spheres in water predicts changes in force direction and resonant magnitude with shell density, elastic properties, hollow ratio and frequency (`0 < ka < 0.7`), using separate low-`ka` and scattering/stress-tensor treatments. This is theoretical/numerical evidence in its stated domain, not a universal theorem; it nevertheless shows why a single test body cannot establish a shared response. A relevant arXiv preprint explicitly develops a “gravitation-like” interpretation as a time-averaged apparent-buoyancy force on a compressible Rayleigh sphere in an infinite inviscid fluid; its assumptions and body-property coupling do not establish a body-independent acoustic gravity field. The claim must be scoped to the declared body and operating domain. See [D00](../D00-document-control.md), [D01](../D01-system-architecture.md), [Gor'kov's primary paper](https://www.mathnet.ru/eng/dan/v140/i1/p88), [Inoue et al.'s primary article](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.05988), the [Wang et al. study](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ultras.2022.106836) and the [Gires et al. preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.01634) (arXiv lists no journal reference). |
| 2. Field nonuniformity may make sustained acceleration difficult | **Valid engineering/science risk; not a contradiction or universal impossibility.** | Inoue et al.'s 40 kHz air experiment reports that each 30 mm sphere's surrounding field changed significantly when the sphere was introduced. Their measured restoring-force maps are position-dependent; five of ten starts more than 5 mm from the levitation point fell within 4 s, while the other five trials remained levitated for 50–144 s. The authors identify a roughly 5 mm stable region and note that perfect asymptotic stability was not reached under their model, which neglected air viscosity. This supports body-in-field characterization in that specific setup; it is not a universal volume limit or evidence about water particles/AURA. It does not prove that every field is too nonuniform: a later case could demonstrate near-constant force over a registered workspace or compensate variation. The small-particle potential-gradient intuition must not be generalized to every scattering regime. Use surface force/torque versus body pose and report both open-loop variation and closed-loop tracking. See the primary paper's Figs. 2–4 and Methods, especially the stated sound-hard-boundary and inviscid-air assumptions ([full text](https://arxiv.org/html/1708.05988v1)). |

Choose a precise initial object and target | **Correct for each falsifiable campaign; do not shrink the whole program to that campaign.** | The target/body/material/medium/environment/trajectory/duration/workspace/observation contract is still open (UNK-003–013). The suggested 0.1–1 g, dimensions and volume are illustrative only, not approved values. DEC-004 explicitly retains larger masses, dimensions and geometries as later research. Freeze one case per campaign while preserving the staged scale track. See [DEC-004](../decisions/DEC-004-staged-mass-and-size-expansion.md), [scale progression](../planning/11-scale-progression.md) and [UNK-011](../registers/unknowns.md). |
| 4. Andrade et al. (PW-15) explicitly note that omitted transducer phase-change and propagation delays could cause small single-input force errors that amplify over a command sequence; in one 2.82 s test the measured oscillation amplitude was about 0.25 mm while the model predicted at most 0.01 mm. This is a bounded air-sphere case, not a universal error estimate, but it supports checking full input sequences against measured output. Reserve the integrated attack/holdout campaign for P8 after the target model and primary observable are frozen. Perturbation levels such as ±5% amplitude are not evidence-based by themselves; derive intervals/distributions from calibration, datasheets or measurement, and label hypothetical stress tests as such. Robustness does not replace independent model checks or measurement validation. | 5. Separate field quality from closed-loop performance | **Correct and directly useful.** | A controller can track a target while compensating a poor or spatially varying field. Record the open-loop field and body-force/torque map for the declared case, then closed-loop trajectory error, actuation and saturation separately. “Field quality” must be an operational statistic, not a visual judgment from pressure plots. For a finite body, surface loading, net force and torque may matter more than pressure maps alone. See [D03](../D03-requirements.md), [P7](../planning/phases/P07-target-control.md) and SC-02's model review. |
| 6. Novelty needs sharper comparison | **Correct; no novelty claim is presently warranted.** | Matouš et al. demonstrated video-feedback position regulation and moving-reference trajectory following of a small ball with an 8×8 ultrasonic array; the floating-ball experiment reports a 50 Hz loop and tracking up to 10 mm/s. Ghanem et al. validated an arbitrary-beam scattering model against lateral-force measurements on millimeter-scale solid spheres in water and steered a 5 mm sphere in 2D on a plate. Their approximately 22% abstract figure is angle discrepancy; overall inferred-force discrepancy is `32.8±58.1%`, with `11.7±8.3%` only in the subset whose friction-to-force ratio is `≤0.6`. Further primary precedents include real-time midair manipulation of a super-wavelength body (PW-11), feedforward levitation of a 98 mm hemispherical shell with 0.608 g mass (PW-12), vertical beat-frequency vibration (PW-13), one-axis beat-driven trajectories (PW-14), experimentally tested cosine-acceleration motion of a 2 mm glass sphere (PW-15), real-time model-based feedforward paths for levitated spheres (PW-16), and experimental phased-array particle transport into microchannels (PW-18). PW-14's model predicts its gravity-dependent mechanism would not occur in microgravity; this is not a microgravity experiment. PW-15 tests a prescribed acceleration-shaped motion strategy, while neither it nor PW-16 reports predeclared acceleration-error acceptance or gravity equivalence. PW-16's trajectory demonstrations use feedforward execution without camera position feedback. PW-17 separately predicts body-property-dependent force direction and magnitude for elastic core-shell spheres in a stated water/standing-wave regime; this is numerical theory, not a universal or experimental result. PW-18 reports phased-array steering into designated microchannels, but full-text access was unavailable and the abstract does not establish acceleration acceptance or gravity equivalence. The results remain body- and apparatus-specific; PW-11/PW-13 are represented by limited publisher previews and PW-18 by its publisher abstract. A traceable acceleration-target comparison in AURA's selected water or later microgravity/scale domain remains a search question, not a novelty finding. ## Proposed operational claim ladder

These are distinctions for future claim review, not a change to D00:

1. **Object-specific acoustic acceleration:** the measured/predicted center-of-mass acceleration of a specified body under specified conditions meets a declared target statistic for a declared time window.
2. **Robust object-specific acceleration:** the same criterion holds across a predeclared uncertainty set and held-out disturbances supported by evidence.
3. **Gravity-like response over a test class/region:** multiple bodies or positions respond within a declared equivalence tolerance and spatial/time domain. This requires a separate operational test; one-object tracking cannot establish it.
4. **Gravity generation:** not an AURA claim. Mechanical acoustic forcing does not create gravitational interaction.

The word “pseudogravity” may be retained as a project label only with the D00 definition and the selected claim level attached. It must not replace the measurable statement in a result.

## Parameterized acceptance template for future review

The following is a candidate test contract, not an adopted requirement or claim. For a registered body `B`, target acceleration field `a_target(x,t)`, evaluation interval `I` of duration `T`, workspace `Ω`, and fixed observation/filtering rule, define

`e_accel(B; I) = sqrt((1/T) ∫_I ||a_obs,B(t) - a_target(x_B(t), t)||² dt)`.

An object-specific result would require `e_accel(B; I) ≤ ε_a`, the body to remain within `Ω` throughout `I`, and the predeclared uncertainty rule to meet its criterion. `ε_a`, `I`, `Ω`, the body/domain, acceleration observation method and uncertainty rule must be frozen before evaluation. This instantiates D01/D03's `error_accel_rms`; it does not add a new metric.

A stronger gravity-like response test must declare a finite body/pose set and spatial/time test domain. It must distinguish **per-body retuned control**, which supports only a body-specific/adaptive capability claim, from a **shared actuation configuration** tested across bodies and positions, which could support a bounded common-response claim if every registered case meets its criterion. Neither finite test establishes universality outside that set. A target, tolerance, test set, shared-actuation condition or criterion is not selected here; owner review remains necessary at CTL-01/D09 claim review.

- Preserve field quality versus control performance as separate future observables; no implementation or metric threshold is set here.
- Add justified local sensitivity checks to P4/P5 when the corresponding model inputs become available; use evidence-derived ranges in P8 and do not adopt the critique's example perturbations as defaults.
