# NUM-03 BEM Kernel Foundation

**Prepared:** 2026-10-05 · **State:** kernel component implemented; boundary-integral solver not implemented or qualified

## Mathematical contract

Use the time dependence `exp(-i omega t)`, so an outgoing free-space Helmholtz Green function is

`G0(x,y) = exp(i k |x-y|) / (4 pi |x-y|)`, with `k=omega/c`.

For a rigid plane `z=0`, the Neumann half-space Green function follows by reflecting the source across the plane:

`GN(x,y) = G0(x,y) + G0(x,y*)`, where `y*=(y_x,y_y,-y_z)`.

This satisfies `partial GN / partial z_x = 0` at `z_x=0` and the outgoing condition. The reflected term is valid for the perfectly rigid plane; formulas for a general impedance plane are a different model. Half-space BEM literature uses Green functions that satisfy the plane condition so the infinite plane does not need to be meshed; see Wu et al. (2015), Sec. 2.1 and its rigid-plane limit, and Li, Wu & Seybert (1994).

For `R=|x-y|`, the observation gradient of one free-space term is

`grad_x G0 = exp(i k R) (i k R - 1) (x-y) / (4 pi R^3)`.

The source gradient follows by differentiating the direct source and the reflected source with the reflection map's Jacobian. The implementation returns the complex kernel and both Cartesian gradients. It rejects points below the plane and coincident direct or image singularities. The supported region is `z>=0`; the physical AURA samples remain strictly in the fluid, above the baffle.

## Relation to the AURA reference problem

The proposed linear decomposition is `p_total = p_piston + p_scattered`. The incident term is the rigidly baffled piston field with prescribed uniform normal face velocity. The scattered field solves the outgoing half-space Helmholtz problem with sphere boundary data equal to the negative incident normal derivative; therefore the total normal derivative on the stationary rigid sphere is zero. This enforces sphere–piston coupling through the exact boundary condition and linear superposition. It does not treat an isolated-sphere solution as if it were coupled.

The next operator must use a uniqueness-enforcing exterior formulation. A Burton–Miller combination of the conventional and normal-derivative boundary integral equations is supported in the literature; Wu et al. give the combined equation and `beta=i/k` choice in Eqs. (8), (11), and (14). That derivation also exposes the hypersingular kernel. Wang, Atalla & Nicolas (1997) derive an axisymmetric Burton–Miller method: for axisymmetric boundary data, the surface equation reduces to integrals along the meridian and over the azimuth; they regularize the hypersingular term with tangential operators and derive special treatment for singular azimuth integrals. Their paper treats a free-space Green function. It does not validate replacing that kernel by AURA's half-space image kernel. The AURA derivation preserves the direct-term singular treatment and separately converges the image contribution, which is smooth for a sphere separated from the plane but becomes near-singular as the gap narrows. A first algebraic adaptation is implemented at the separated ring-kernel level; equation signs, jump factors and coupling coefficient remain to be checked under AURA's `exp(-iwt)` convention before use.

The AURA geometry and drive are rotationally symmetric about the piston axis only while the piston velocity is uniform, the sphere is centered on that axis, and the baffle is ideal and infinite. Under those declared assumptions, only the zero azimuthal Fourier mode is required. Breaking any one of them requires restoring nonzero azimuthal modes or changing the model; the current kernel component does not handle either case.

## Normal derivatives and Burton–Miller kernel split

For a separated free-space point pair, let `d=x-y`, `R=|d|`, and `E=exp(i k R)`. The mixed derivative is

`d²G0/(dn_x dn_y) = -A (n_x·n_y) - B (n_x·d)(n_y·d)`,

where `A=E(i k R-1)/(4 pi R^3)` and `B=E(3-3 i k R-(kR)^2)/(4 pi R^5)`. For the image term, reflect both the source position and source normal with `J=diag(1,1,-1)`; the mixed derivative uses source normal `J n_y`. The implemented function accepts the physical source normal and applies this chain-rule transformation internally. It rejects the direct diagonal.

Wang, Atalla & Nicolas (1997), Eq. (6), give the Maue regularization for the free-space hypersingular operator with their `exp(-i k R)` Green function. Conjugating to AURA's `exp(+i k R)` convention leaves the real `k²` factor and product structure unchanged. The direct-term identity used as the candidate is

`integral_S p(Q) d²G0/(dn_P dn_Q) dS_Q = integral_S [k² (n_P·n_Q) p(Q) G0 + (n_P×grad_P G0)·(n_Q×grad_Q p)] dS_Q`.

The half-space Green function is a direct term plus an image. Maue regularization is applied here only to the singular direct term. At a strictly positive sphere–plane gap, the image term has no source/field diagonal on the physical sphere, so it is evaluated by the mixed-normal image kernel with the reflected source normal. It becomes near-singular as the gap narrows and needs its own near-singular quadrature study. This split is an algebraic adaptation candidate; the complete surface identity, jumps, Burton–Miller equation signs and coupling coefficient have not yet been independently checked for the AURA boundary-value problem.

For the axisymmetric zero mode, `n=(n_r,n_z)` and the selected unit meridian tangent is `tau=(-n_z,n_r)`, giving `n×grad_S p = -(dp/ds)e_phi`. The direct Maue ring integral analytically contracts the static Laplace contribution. It uses the exact scalar ring integral `I0`, the cosine-weighted moment `Ic=integral(cos(phi) G_L dphi)`, its field-height derivative, and the source-height gradient of `I0`: `k² p (n_fr n_sr Ic + n_fz n_sz I0) + p_s (n_fr partial_zf Ic + n_fz partial_zs I0)`. The remaining dynamic Green and gradient terms are midpoint-integrated as smooth Helmholtz-minus-Laplace residuals with compensated accumulation. The image mixed-normal term remains a separate image-kernel integral. `integrate_neumann_ring_burton_miller_terms_zero_mode` returns these two azimuth integrals without the `r ds` surface Jacobian and rejects coincident meridional rings. It does not perform the singular meridian integration, add boundary jump terms, assemble a Burton–Miller equation, or allocate a matrix.

The exact cosine moment follows from the complete elliptic integrals `K(m)` and `E(m)` as `Ic=(2(K-E)/m-K)/(π S)`, where `S=sqrt((r_f+r_s)^2+dz^2)` and `m=4 r_f r_s/S^2`; its height derivative differentiates both `m` and `S`. A small-`m` series avoids cancellation near the axis. The implemented moment and derivative agree with a 32,768-point independent angular midpoint sum for the tested separated ring. A separate near-diagonal check (`H=0.1 mm`, `theta_f=175°`, `theta_s=175.1°`, `f=25,230 Hz`) compares the static-subtracted 1,024-point direct Maue ring result with an independently coded 32,768-point full Helmholtz-kernel midpoint sum, including the complete pointwise gradient contraction. The test passes its relative tolerance `2e-11`. This is one finite-grid comparison, not an error bound, convergence proof or panel-integral validation.

For a local diagonal probe, set `p=0` and constant meridional tangent derivative `p_s=1` on the sphere. With source offset `Δs=a Δtheta` from a field point at `theta=175°`, the product `Δs * direct_ring` approaches `1/(2π r_f)` as the offset decreases from `0.1°` to `0.02°`; the smallest offset differs by about 1.3%. Since the physical meridian integral later multiplies by `r_s ds`, its leading local kernel is `1/(2π Δs)` in arclength, interpreted in the Cauchy principal-value sense. The offset sweep is an asymptotic diagnostic for this one sphere point, not an integration rule or error bound. A candidate panel method must subtract this leading term, integrate its principal-value panel contribution analytically, and separately test the residual and curvature terms against exact sphere modes.

The static scalar ring has a separate weak logarithmic singularity. From `I_L=K(k)/(π S)`, `k'=sqrt((r_f-r_s)^2+dz^2)/S`, the local smooth-surface limits `S≈2r_f` and `k'≈|Δs|/(2r_f)`, and the complete-elliptic asymptotic `K(k)≈ln(4/k')`, it follows that `r_s I_L≈(1/(2π)) ln(8r_f/|Δs|)`. A sphere offset sweep at `theta=175°` approaches this leading expression monotonically; the `0.01°` case differs by about `0.11%`. The asymptotic formula is scoped to positive radius and a smooth meridian away from its endpoints. The general elliptic expansion is in DLMF §19.12; the sphere numbers check the geometry-specific substitution, not an error bound.

These leading terms define a candidate product-integration split, not an accepted panel algorithm. Let `ξ=s_source-s_field`, and let `g` be a smooth density factor. For a panel `[a,b]` containing an interior collocation point `s0`, write

`PV integral_a^b g(s)/(2π(s-s0)) ds = g(s0)/(2π) ln((b-s0)/(s0-a)) + integral_a^b [g(s)-g(s0)]/(2π(s-s0)) ds`.

The first term is exact and the quotient in the second term has a removable limit for differentiable `g`. For a leading logarithmic panel term, use the exact primitive `F(ξ)=ξ(ln(L/|ξ|)+1)` for `ln(L/|ξ|)` and subtract the local density value before integrating the remainder. Optional linear Taylor subtraction further smooths both residuals when the shape-function derivative is known; its analytic principal-value and log moments are restored exactly. The local scale `L` (for example `8r_f`) must be used consistently in the subtracted and restored terms; it cancels from the complete integral. The scalar `k²` coefficient, normal dot product, surface-Jacobian variation, curvature corrections and dynamic residual remain explicit in the remainder. Endpoint collocation, panel joins and the image term need separate rules.

`src/aura/fields/_bem_singular.py` implements these one-dimensional primitives. Independent polynomial antiderivatives check the principal-value and log helpers. A one-panel full-meridian sphere prototype applies the direct ring Maue term for degrees `l=0,1`, subtracts the local singular terms, and integrates the curved/dynamic residual at Gauss orders 16/32/64 with 1,024 azimuth points per ring. The order-64 results match the independent exact-sphere hypersingular eigenvalues within the test's `2e-4` relative tolerance; each refinement reduces observed error by at least factors of 2 and 3. This validates this bounded direct free-space sphere calculation only. It does not validate panel endpoints or joins, general shape functions, the image contribution, mesh assembly, the half-space Burton–Miller equation, or an AURA field. Do not infer general convergence from these three tested orders.

The same direct full-meridian sphere calculation is now evaluated with the field collocation at the north pole, where the meridional coordinate meets the axis and the interior-point principal-value/log decomposition is inapplicable. The static source-gradient and cosine-moment helpers use their exact zero-radius limits instead of forming a complementary modulus that can exceed one by rounding. Degrees `l=0,1` match the exact sphere hypersingular eigenvalues within `3e-13` relative at Gauss orders 32 and 64. This covers this polar collocation limit; it does not establish arbitrary panel endpoint or join rules.

### Panel-join probe — bounded piecewise-smooth case

The one-panel primitives interpret an interior singular point as the shared collocation point of the left and right subintervals. A join probe uses a continuous piecewise-linear density with different one-sided slopes. The Cauchy principal value agrees with its exact piecewise antiderivative to `2e-14`; the logarithmic integral converges toward its exact piecewise antiderivative by approximately a factor of 16 for each doubling from Gauss order 16 through 256, reaching an absolute difference of `1.3e-10` at order 256. This establishes behavior for that continuous kink only, not for discontinuous densities, arbitrary curved panels, or an error bound. A Cauchy singularity at an outer endpoint remains rejected because its one-sided integral diverges for nonzero density; both sides of an internal join must be combined to take the principal value. The logarithmic helper now supports either outer endpoint, where the singularity is integrable; constant-plus-linear densities at both endpoints match independent primitives within `2e-14` using order 8. These endpoint checks are analytic primitive checks only, not curved-panel or global convergence evidence.

The import is now lazy in both the panel helper and its test. The two failed checks were preserved in the turn's CI output; the focused rerun passed all 36 tests. This software failure did not represent a scientific run. A later complete run passed after the polar-limit correction; the subsequent join and log-endpoint tests require the current full-workflow rerun.

Focused checks also include centered differences of the mixed-normal kernel along the physical source normal, the rigid-plane zero-normal-derivative limit, the zero-radius ring limit for the direct Maue term, an independent angular finite-difference check of the image term, the free-space Maue identity for two smooth densities on an analytic sphere, and degree-0/1 exact-sphere checks of the CBIE, HBIE jumps and Burton–Miller combination. For the BIE candidate, `n_E` points outward from the exterior acoustic domain into the sphere, matching Wu et al.; `q_s=∂_{n_E}p_s=-∂_{n_E}p_inc`. With smooth-surface jumps, the derived combined equation is `(1/2 I + K_N + beta H_N) p_s = (V_N + beta K'_N - beta/2 I) q_s`, using `beta=i/k` under `exp(-i omega t)`. Here `V_N` is the single-layer operator, `K_N` the principal-value source-normal derivative, `K'_N` the principal-value field-normal derivative, and `H_N` the hypersingular finite-part operator. The two sphere modes verify this free-space operator sign/jump mapping only; they do not verify inserting the half-space image kernel into the complete equation. Direct free-space singular subtraction now has one-panel sphere prototypes for interior and polar collocation plus a continuous piecewise-linear join probe. Outer Cauchy endpoints remain unsupported as one-sided integrals; the logarithmic helper now integrates both outer endpoints for constant-plus-linear densities. ## Quadrature design boundary (2026-10-06)

The direct and image kernels need distinct treatments, with evidence kept separate:

* **Direct diagonal, singular:** Maue regularization lowers the hypersingular operator but does not make its diagonal ring integrand ordinary smooth data. The azimuthal Fourier reduction in Wang, Atalla & Nicolas (1997), Sec. II, separates the Helmholtz kernel into a frequency-dependent regular part and a static singular part (their Eq. 22), then evaluates singular circumferential Fourier integrals with recurrences based on complete elliptic integrals. As a first scalar component, the static Green ring integral has the exact zero-mode form `I_L = K(k)/(pi*S)`, with `S=sqrt((r_f+r_s)^2+dz^2)`, `k^2=4 r_f r_s/S^2`, and complementary modulus `k'=sqrt((r_f-r_s)^2+dz^2)/S`. It follows directly by substituting `R^2=S^2(1-k^2 cos^2(phi/2))` into the angular integral. The implementation computes `K(k)=pi/(2 AGM(1,k'))` using the DLMF AGM relation, avoiding subtraction in `1-k^2`. Coincident meridional rings have `k'=0`, so the ring integral still diverges logarithmically; this helper rejects that case for separate meridional treatment. This is only the static scalar Green term: the dynamic Helmholtz remainder, derivative kernels, Maue product, and meridian-panel diagonal are not thereby integrated or qualified. The wider Wang recurrence remains the closest source-matched candidate for those terms. Its free-space recurrence cannot be assumed to validate the complete half-space formulation.
* **Image contribution, near-singular:** for positive sphere-plane clearance the reflected source does not coincide with the physical surface, so its kernel is finite. As clearance shrinks, the angular integrand can form a narrow peak near equal azimuth. Johnston & Elliott (2005) show that a sinh coordinate transform clusters quadrature points around a projected near-singular point, using its distance to the element; this motivates a gap-aware transform, but their result is for element integrals and is not an off-the-shelf proof for a periodic ring. Keep ordinary periodic quadrature as a baseline, compare a ring-adapted gap-scaled transform against a separately implemented high-accuracy reference over declared clearance/radius ratios, and record error versus work. Do not merge image error with direct singular error.

The first deterministic study will use the already frozen NUM-01 air inputs: `f=25,230 Hz`, `c=346 m/s`, sphere radius `a=0.025 m`, and positive gaps `H={0.1,10,20,29.9} mm`; retain NUM-01's density and `exp(-i omega t)` convention. With polar angle `theta` from +z, parameterize the lower sphere meridian as `r=a sin(theta)`, `z=H+a(1+cos(theta))`, and derive `k=2 pi f/c` from those exact inputs. For the direct term, probe the meridian diagonal and symmetric neighboring source locations about `theta=pi/2`; use densities `p=1` and `p=cos(theta)` so the latter exercises the tangential derivative. For the image term, evaluate same- and neighboring-meridian ring pairs at `theta={150°,170°,175°,179°}` across all four clearances; the positive image separation is `z_field+z_source`, so the small-gap cases expose the narrow angular scale without introducing an actual singularity. The image-only ring routine now has an experimental split-and-scale midpoint option. The first probe compares the ordinary periodic midpoint rule at 16/64/128 points with this option and a separately coded 65,536/131,072-point midpoint reference. Direct singular tests compare both the static scalar part and its mixed-normal derivative to analytic elliptic-integral controls. These levels are an initial diagnostic design, not a convergence or accuracy claim.

For positive-radius image rings, the exact azimuthal separation is `R_img(phi)^2 = delta^2 + 4 r_field r_source sin^2(phi/2)`, where `delta^2=(r_field-r_source)^2+(z_field+z_source)^2`. This identifies the near-peak width `phi_c=delta/sqrt(r_field*r_source)` when `phi_c << 1`; it is a scale estimate, not an error bound. A transparent ring-specific candidate is to split at `phi=±min(pi,L phi_c)`, scale the central panel with `u=phi/phi_c`, and integrate the smooth outer arcs separately. Vary `L` and central/outer orders independently; if either ring radius is zero or `phi_c` is not small, use the regular full-period rule. The image test must compare the complete complex mixed-normal integrand, since the radial and axial normal factors can change its peak and cancellation relative to the Green-function distance alone. This split-and-scale candidate is distinct from Johnston & Elliott's element sinh map and must be validated on its own.

### Direct static mixed-normal ring kernel — analytic control

For separated meridional rings, the zero-mode static potential is `V=K(m)/(pi*S)`, where `S=sqrt(A)`, `A=(r_f+r_s)^2+(z_f-z_s)^2`, and `m=4 r_f r_s/A` is the Legendre parameter (`m=k^2`, not the modulus). Differentiating this scalar expression twice gives the meridian Hessian. The implementation uses `K_m = [E/(1-m)-K]/(2m)` and differentiates that identity for `K_mm`; the derivative identities follow from DLMF §19.4 after converting from modulus `k` to parameter `m`. For small `m`, the code uses the Maclaurin coefficients of `K` and their termwise derivatives to avoid cancellation and cover zero-radius limits. The desired normal integral is the contraction `n_fr n_sr V_(r_f r_s) + n_fr n_sz V_(r_f z_s) + n_fz n_sr V_(z_f r_s) + n_fz n_sz V_(z_f z_s)`.

For the NUM-01 sphere case at `H=0.1 mm`, `theta_field=175°`, and `theta_source=175.1°`, the analytic contraction was `3.876385732351382e10 m^-3`. A separately coded pointwise static mixed-normal kernel integrated by periodic midpoint changed by less than `7e-5 m^-3` between 32,768 and 65,536 azimuth samples; its 65,536-point value differed from the analytic contraction by about `6.3e-2 m^-3` (relative `1.6e-12`). Two zero-radius axis limits also matched the independent angular expression. These are implementation cross-checks for a noncoincident ring pair and axis limits, not an error bound, meridian singular quadrature, Maue verification, half-space image validation, assembled operator, or field result. The pointwise control shares the differentiated physical kernel formula but not the elliptic reduction, so it is an independent integration route rather than independent physics.

The same elliptic potential now supplies exact static field/source meridian gradients. For the dynamic Helmholtz gradient, the implementation adds the static gradient to midpoint integration of the smooth residual `[exp(i k R)(i k R-1)+1] d/(4 pi R^3)` and its source-coordinate projection. The residual's real and imaginary numerators use cancellation-resistant trigonometric forms. On the `175°/175.1°`, `0.1 mm` pair, the 512-point split result differed from a separately coded 32,768-point full-gradient sum by a maximum component-relative `5.25e-9`; at 1,024 points that finite-grid difference fell to `1.58e-12`. The 2,048-point result differed by `1.69e-12`, showing a binary64 floor for this comparator. The initial residual implementation had incorrect trigonometric signs and disagreed with the full-gradient sum; that attempt was rejected, corrected, and the test now records that 512 samples did not meet its comparison tolerance. Static field/source gradients also match a direct static angular sum on this pair. These are one-pair quadrature comparisons, not global error bounds. They do not integrate the meridional diagonal, qualify the full Maue product, validate the half-space image identity, assemble or solve a BIE, or establish an AURA field.

The elliptic derivatives use the directly computed complementary modulus `q=sqrt((r_f-r_s)^2+(z_f-z_s)^2)/S` and differentiate `q^2`, rather than form `1-m` by subtracting from a rounded parameter. This distinction matters when separated rings are extremely close: at equal `10 mm` radii and a `1e-12 m` axial gap, the Legendre parameter rounds to one in binary64. The static first-gradient and mixed-normal values remain finite and agree with their leading logarithmic-kernel asymptotics to a maximum relative difference of `2.7e-6`. This is a numerical-conditioning regression for one asymptotic case, not an accuracy guarantee at arbitrary separations.
